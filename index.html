AN EXPLORATION OF PHYSICAL AI EDUCATION

How do we teach AI that meets the physical world?

A robot can follow a line in a demo. The deeper lesson starts when students ask why it drifted, what it sensed, and how the next action changed what it could see.

01 / THE IDEA

WHAT MAKES THIS A DISTINCT LEARNING EXPERIENCE?

It’s about what students investigate.

Physical AI education asks students to study how sensing, decisions, and actions work together in a real environment.

Putting AI code on a robot does not automatically make that relationship visible. Students need to gather evidence from physical interaction and explain how it affects the system’s behavior.

01

Study intelligent behavior

AI behavior is a main learning goal, rather than only assembling or programming hardware.

02

Use live physical evidence

Students produce or interpret evidence from sensing, action, or environmental interaction whose outcome is not fully predictable.

03

Explain the feedback loop

Learning goals or assessment explicitly address embodied sensing and how actions change later observations.

04

Face a real constraint

Uncertainty, limited resources, or responsibility meaningfully changes the analysis or design.

What about simulation? It is valuable for preparation, comparison, and access. Simulation or replay alone cannot provide live physical evidence; a remote lab can when students direct and interpret live interaction.

03 / IN THE CLASSROOM

Choose a complication. See how a short demonstration becomes a question students can investigate.

UNCERTAINTY / PERCEPTION

The camera sees a different line.

A shadow changes the image. The robot turns too late, moves off the line, and its next camera view changes again.

ASK STUDENTS TO…

Compare camera images and steering decisions under different lighting. Run repeated trials and explain how changed observations led to changed actions.

COLLECT EVIDENCE

Images or sensor readings, a time-aligned action trace, and results from repeated runs.

QUESTIONS FOR FUTURE STUDIES

A starting point for research.

These questions invite future studies. The framework is conceptual; its effects on learning have not been established.

Q / 01

How do we measure understanding?

Can assessments reveal whether students can explain connections across sensing, action, and constraints?

Q / 02

How much physical interaction helps?

Which learning goals need live hardware, and which can be prepared through simulation or data replay?

Q / 03

Who can access the experience?

How do equipment time, setup, maintenance, and student needs affect opportunity to learn?
